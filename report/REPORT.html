0. One-paragraph summary

I ran the authors' pipeline unchanged, reproduced its non-LLM numbers exactly (Pass@1 79.4%, oracle 92.1% on all 89 Terminal-Bench 2.1 tasks), and then attempted the paper's best-of-3 verifier-selection result on a 12-task subset of the 24 tasks where selection can matter. The pipeline runs end to end with no errors and selection beats the agent's Pass@1 on the subset (50.0% → 91.7%), so the direction of the claim reproduces; the paper's 86.5%±1.1% magnitude is not comparable, because the verifier model differs, I ran 3 seeds and a subset. The most interesting finding is a negative one: on hosted DeepSeek the logprob distribution at the score token is one-hot (97% of scores sit within 1e-3 scale points of a single letter), so the paper's "expectation over the full score distribution" is inert and the method effectively runs as LLM-as-a-Judge with a 20-point letter. My modification — coarsening that scale from 20 letters to 5 — therefore tests the one component that survives, and finds no detectable difference at this sample size, which is what the one-hot measurement predicts.

1. Project and what I tried to reproduce

System. LLM-as-a-Verifier turns a verifier model into a fine-grained reward model. Given a task and two candidate agent trajectories, it asks the model to score each on one criterion using a 20-token letter scale (A–T), then replaces the usual single sampled label with the expectation of the score over the model's logprob distribution at that token, E[φ(v)] = Σg p(vg)·φ(vg). Those pairwise rewards feed a Probabilistic Pivot Tournament (ring pass, then pivot rounds) that selects the best of N rollouts in O(Nk) comparisons instead of O(N²). It is a genuine agentic system: a multi-stage loop over trajectories, criteria and repeated evaluations, with its own planner (pivot rounds), cache and cost accounting.

Target. The paper's self-verification on Terminal-Bench 2.1 row — five mini-swe-agent rollouts per task, re-ranked by the verifier:

configurationPass@1LLM-as-a-Verifieroracle
best-of-3 (paper, mean ± std over 3 seeds)79.4%86.5% ± 1.1%92.1%

Metric definitions (as in the repo's scripts/run.py): Pass@1 = mean ground-truth reward over all trials; verifier = fraction of tasks whose selected trial succeeds; oracle = fraction of tasks with at least one successful trial. Only the best-of-3 configuration is reproduced (the best-of-5 row is used as a second free check of the data, §4).

3. Scope: why a subset, and why this one

Of the 89 tasks, only the ones whose rollouts disagree can change under selection: 58 tasks are all-pass and 7 are all-fail, so they are decided before the verifier is called. That leaves 24 swing tasks. A full best-of-3 run therefore needs 24 × 5 directed comparisons (4 when a pivot-round pair coincides with a ring pair) × 3 criteria × K=2 ≈ 720 verifier calls, and the repository's own best-of-5 run needs 1200 more.

I scoped to the first 12 swing tasks in sorted order — a mechanical rule, not a difficulty-based choice — because it kept a run inside both the API budget and a two-day deadline while leaving enough tasks for the claim to be visible. Two honest caveats: (i) task-level accuracy moves in steps of 8.3 points, and (ii) the seed count is bounded by budget, not by the pipeline (§4). The report therefore also gives comparison-level and trial-level statistics (31 disagreeing comparisons, 36 scored trials), which have more samples than the task count.

The subset is not easier or harder than the rest in the way that matters: its Pass@1 is 50.0% against 52.9% for all 24 swing tasks. Scoping loses statistical power, not coverage of the phenomenon.

Reading. On the subset, Pass@1 is 50.0% and the verifier selects a successful rollout on 11 of 12 tasks (91.7%), i.e. 91.7% against an oracle of 100.0% — 1 task(s) below the oracle in this configuration. At the comparison level the verifier prefers the successful rollout in 28/31 comparisons where the two trials disagree (90.3%), and the trial-level AUC of the verifier reward against the binary reward is 0.861 (18 successful vs 18 failed trials). That is the paper's qualitative claim — a frozen verifier re-ranks rollouts better than chance — and it holds on this data. The gap to 86.5% is not evidence against the paper: the model is different, and the subset is small: selection is 11/12 in all 3 seeds (91.7% ± 0.0), while the finer metrics move — pairwise preference 90.9% ± 2.7, trial AUC 0.841 ± 0.022 — which is the resolution a 12-task count cannot show.

How much can a 12-task sample actually say? Exact sign tests against the Pass@1 baseline (p = 0.5 per task) answer this precisely. At task level, 11 of 12 selections being correct gives p = 0.0032; the comparison-level count (28/31 disagreeing comparisons preferring the successful rollout) gives p = 2.3e-06; and the trial-level AUC of 0.861 over 18 successful vs 18 failed trials corresponds to z = 3.70, p = 2.1e-04. So the subset is more than enough to establish direction and discrimination, and far too small for a magnitude claim: at n = 12 a one-task difference is invisible (p = 0.39), while a two-task difference would have been detected (p = 0.019). That bound is exactly what §6.3 leans on.

6. The modification, and the finding that shaped it

6.1 What I changed

Score-scale granularity: 20 letters (A–T) → 5 letters (A–E). One isolated change, in llm_verifier/fine_grained_reward.py: GRANULARITY is read from LLM_VERIFIER_GRANULARITY and the scale (band description + valid score tokens) is built from it. Unset, the scale is byte-identical to upstream — I verified that the A–T description string and the 40 valid tokens (upper + lower case) are unchanged — so the reproduction path above is untouched. extract_score already renormalises by the min/max of the valid tokens, so rewards stay in [0, 1] and the tournament, cache and metric code needed no change. The two configurations never share a score cache (separate cache files), so no score is reused across the ablation.

6.2 Why that modification

Fine-grained scoring granularity is the paper's headline mechanism, and it is the component most at risk on a hosted API — which is exactly what I found while profiling calls. On the 576 cached scores from this configuration at g=20, 97.2% sit within 1e-3 scale points of a pure single-letter value (median deviation 7.4e-7, i.e. essentially zero), and the measured top-token probability at the score position is ≈0.99999:

Figure 2. Distance of every cached score from the nearest single-letter value. A distribution with real mass spread would put substantial mass near 0.5 scale points; instead everything piles up at 0.

Consequence: E[φ(v)] ≈ φ(argmax v), so the "expectation over the distribution" step is inert and the method degrades to LLM-as-a-Judge with a 20-point label. That reshapes the modification into a cleaner question: if the only surviving signal is the label alphabet, does its resolution matter at all? A second endpoint I tested (a DeepSeek-compatible host that strips token logprobs) returns scores on exactly the same grid through the text fallback — it produced no sub-grid values either — although it is served by a different model build and disagreed with the first backend on the letter of one probe pair (E vs B for the same trajectory, ordering preserved). Two lessons: the fine-grained path is unavailable there too, and backends must not be mixed inside one configuration, which is why every configuration here is single-backend.

6.3 What changed

Coarsening the scale from 20 letters to 5 leaves task-level selection unchanged, and this now holds under repetition: the baseline selects a successful rollout on 11 of 12 tasks in all three seeds (91.7% ± 0.0), and the ablation repeats that 11 of 12 on the shared seed. The finer statistics show the seed noise the task counting is too coarse to resolve — pairwise preference accuracy is 90.3 / 88.6 / 93.9 across seeds (90.9 ± 2.7) and trial AUC is 0.861 / 0.843 / 0.818 (0.841 ± 0.022) — while the g5 run, on the same 282 of 288 comparisons, lands within that band (90.6% pairwise, 0.852 AUC). At vote level the ablation does move things: 21/186 informative comparisons flip (11.3%) against 29/96 uninformative ones (30.2%), and the margin widens from 0.317 to 0.369. Because a task decision aggregates 30 votes, those flips cancel. The honest claim is 'no difference larger than about two tasks', which is what a one-hot score distribution predicts.

Figure 3. Distribution of verifier rewards per configuration; red ticks are the g-point grid. Both pile up on the grid, which is why coarsening the scale mostly relabels the same ordering.

The ablation did move the votes, though. On the 282 comparisons that were scored under both configurations, the mean absolute score change is 0.111 (median 0.053), the preference margin widens from 0.317 to 0.369, and the number of distinct score values collapses from 22 to 8. More tellingly, the pairwise preference flips on 21 of the 186 comparisons whose two trials actually disagree (11.3%), against 29 of 96 comparisons whose trials share the same outcome (30.2%). Five-fold coarsening therefore rewrites roughly one vote in nine where it matters and almost one in three where it cannot matter — and because a task's decision aggregates 30 votes (5 directed comparisons × 3 criteria × K=2), the flips cancel instead of changing the winner. The tournament is robust to the label alphabet, not blind to it.

What this null result can and cannot mean. At n = 12, a one-task difference is undetectable (exact binomial p = 0.39); a two-task difference would have shown up (p = 0.019). The observed difference is zero tasks, with identical pairwise accuracy and AUC within 0.01. The correct claim is therefore "no difference larger than about two tasks", which — given that the score distribution is one-hot, so the ablation only rewrites the same preference in a coarser alphabet — is the outcome the mechanism predicts.

Cost side. The g5 answer block is shorter (five valid tokens instead of twenty, a shorter scale description), but the prompt is dominated by the two trajectories — tens of thousands of tokens — so the saving is in the noise: measured cost per call stayed at ≈ ¥0.01 on both configurations. Granularity buys resolution, not tokens.

What is reproducible: the code, the data, the evaluation protocol and the non-LLM numbers — all of it, quickly, on a laptop. What is not: the published selection magnitude under a different verifier, and the fine-grained mechanism itself on any backend whose score-token distribution is one-hot or unavailable. The paper's central architectural claim (a cheap frozen verifier beats random selection over rollouts) is well supported by my data; its fine-grained implementation detail is the part that a hosted API cannot deliver.

9. Recommendations to future users

  1. Measure the score distribution before trusting the fine-grained reward. One cached batch is enough: if E[φ] sits within 1e-3 of φ(argmax), the method is a judge with an ordinal label, and criteria/scale ablations will be null results by construction.
  2. Scope by swing tasks. 73% of this benchmark cannot change under selection; a reproduction that samples all tasks uniformly spends most of its budget on decided tasks.
  3. Keep the score cache on disk and never cache a failure. Across two balance/concurrency outages this turned a 2-hour run into an incremental one, and it makes "re-run until zero errors" a safe recovery procedure.
  4. Pin the data by hash. The dataset is the part that reproduces perfectly; a git clone through a flaky network silently rots it.
  5. Set max_workers from your key's limits, not from the repo default (500–2000): an over-parallel run fails as 0.5/0.5 ties, which look like a bad verifier rather than a bad configuration.
